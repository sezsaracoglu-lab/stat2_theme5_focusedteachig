📜 The rules of the game

Ten rules that govern the whole method. Each step then has two or three of its own, behind the 📜 chip inside that step.

  1. Scale data, assumptions met — or you are in the wrong lecture. Nominal data means frequencies and χ²; ordinal means ranks and Spearman’s rₛ. Correlation and regression need two quantitative variables with the distributional assumptions met.
  2. The plot comes before the number. Direction, form, strength, outliers — said out loud, in that order. r cannot see form, and it is not resistant to outliers, so the picture is the only guard against both.
  3. SP carries the sign; the denominator only removes the units. r = SP ⁄ √(SS(x)·SS(y)). SS(x) and SS(y) can never be negative, so the sign of r is entirely the sign of SP. Divide SP by SS(x) instead and you have the slope, not the correlation.
  4. Least squares means squared VERTICAL gaps. The line minimises Σ(Y − Ŷ)², measured straight up and down, because a vertical gap is exactly a prediction error in Y. And (X̄, Ȳ) is always on the line — a free check.
  5. A hat means a prediction. Ŷ is never an observed value. Every interpretation of b₀ and b₁ is about the PREDICTED outcome, on average, across people — not a promise to any individual.
  6. The four assumptions are about the residuals. Linear relation · Y normal at every X · equal variance at every X · independent observations. Nothing assumes X is normal, and nothing assumes Y is normal overall.
  7. Every coefficient is tested against its OWN standard error. t = b ⁄ SE(b) on df = n − p − 1. Slope sizes are not comparable across variables unless the predictors share a scale and a spread, because each b carries the units of its own predictor. Beta and t are the comparable figures.
  8. Mean squares before F, always. SST = SSM + SSE. Divide each by its own df, then F = MSM ⁄ MSE on df (p, n − p − 1). Dividing the sums of squares directly gives a number that means nothing.
  9. With one predictor, everything is the same test. F = t², Beta = r, R² = Beta², and the two p-values match. All four identities break the moment a second predictor arrives — which is the point of the second half of the lecture.
  10. R² only ever rises, so a rise is not evidence. Adding any predictor, however useless, cannot lower R². That is why adjusted R² and the F change test exist, and why “it went up” is never an answer.

  • Describe a scatterplot in the four required terms — direction, form, strength, outliers — and say which of them r is blind to.

  • Interpret r² as a share of variation, and use a residual plot to judge whether a straight line was the right shape.
  • Read an SPSS Coefficients, ANOVA and Model Summary table: recover n, write the equation, and find the df for each test.

🏛️ The whole method in one paragraph

Decide whether you are exploring or explaining, and check that the data are scale with the assumptions met. Look at the plot and say four things: direction, form, strength, outliers. If the form is straight, measure the togetherness with r = SP ⁄ √(SS(x)·SS(y)), and turn the same SP into a line: b₁ = SP ⁄ SS(x), b₀ = Ȳ − b₁X̄, chosen because they minimise the squared vertical gaps. Check the four assumptions on the residuals, then ask how much the line explains (r² = SSM ⁄ SST) and whether a straight line was the right shape at all (the residual plot). Now test it. Each coefficient against its own standard error: t = b ⁄ SE(b) on df = n − p − 1, with a confidence interval saying the same thing. The model as a whole by splitting the variance: SST = SSM + SSE, each divided by its own df, F = MSM ⁄ MSE — which, with one predictor, is exactly t². Then say how useful it is: s = √(SSE ⁄ df error) is the average prediction error in the units of Y, and it builds two intervals — a narrow one for the average y at a given x, a wider one for one new case. Finally, add a second predictor and every coefficient becomes partial — “with the other held constant” — the Betas replace the raw b’s for comparison, R becomes the correlation between predicted and observed scores, and any further block has to prove itself with an F change test, because R² rises whether the predictor was worth adding or not.

In words

The deck opens and closes on the same flowchart, and every exam question secretly starts there. Two things settle it: what kind of numbers you have, and what you want from them. Nominal data means frequencies, ordinal data means ranks, and only scale data with the distributional assumptions met reaches this lecture at all. Then slide 3 asks the question that splits the chapter in two — do you want to explore a relationship, or show that one variable explains variation in another?

  • Slide 5’s five steps run the whole lecture, in order: question · visualisation · hypothesis test · reliability of the estimate · how good is the model.
  • One predictor → simple regression. Two or more → multiple regression, which is the second half of the deck.

Rules of the game for step 1

  • Nominal data means frequencies and χ² or phi; ordinal means ranks and Spearman’s rₛ. Only SCALE data with the distributional assumptions met reaches correlation and regression.
  • Correlation is symmetric — there is no first variable. Regression is not: you must nominate a predictor and an outcome, and that choice is yours to justify, not the statistics’.
  • In this whole lecture every variable is quantitative (slide 13’s small print). A nominal predictor is a later course.

Say it to a friend

“Before you pick a test you check two things: what kind of numbers you have, and what you want from them. Two continuous variables and you want to describe how they move together — that’s correlation. You want to predict one from the other — that’s regression. And once you’ve fitted a line, you still have to ask whether it’s real and how accurate it is.”

Key insight

Correlation and regression run the same arithmetic. What separates them is the question you brought to the data — and only regression lets you put a value of x in and get a number out.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Seeing the word “relationship” in a question and reaching straight for r. If the question says predict, estimate or explain, it wants a regression — and the answer has units.

Check yourself

A researcher measures hours of sleep and exam score on 40 students, and asks whether exam score can be predicted from sleep. Which analysis, and why?
Simple linear regression. Both variables are scale, and “predicted from” nominates sleep as the predictor and exam score as the outcome — an asymmetric question. Correlation would answer a weaker question: how tightly the two move together, with no direction.
Why can the same two columns of numbers give you one correlation but two regression lines?
r treats the variables equally, so swapping them changes nothing. Least squares minimises VERTICAL distances, and which direction counts as vertical depends on which variable sits on the y axis. The two lines coincide only when |r| = 1.

📈 Look at the picture first

Direction, form, strength, outliers — and the one shape that breaks r.

⬇ builds ona named analysis from step 1⬆ hands uppermission to summarise the cloud with one number — or a warning not to
  • ▸Direction. Rising is positive, falling is negative — beer sales and coffee sales against temperature are the textbook pair.
  • ▸Form. Straight, curved or clustered. This is the one thing r cannot see.
  • ▸Strength. How tightly the points hug the form — the WIDTH of the band, not its steepness.
🪝 memory hookr is a ruler that only measures straight things. Hand it a curve and it will still give you a number — a meaningless one.

In words

Slides 8 to 10 spend three slides on pictures before a single coefficient is computed, and that order is deliberate. Four things get said out loud about any scatterplot: direction, form, strength, outliers. Three of them r can handle. The fourth — form — it cannot see at all, and slide 10 exists to make that point: a clear curve between x and y still gets summarised by r as if it were a line.

  • Outliers. Points sitting apart from the pattern. r is not resistant to them: a few can move it a long way.
  • A clear curve still gets summarised by r as if it were a line. That summary is simply wrong, however neat the number looks.

Rules of the game for step 2

  • Say four things out loud about every scatterplot — direction, form, strength, outliers — and in that order, because the second one can cancel the whole analysis.
  • Strength is the WIDTH of the band, not the STEEPNESS of it. A steep line through a loose cloud is a weak relationship with a big slope.
  • If the form is not straight, stop. Every number downstream — r, b₁, r², F, p — is computed as though it were.

Say it to a friend

“Before computing anything I look at the picture and say three things out loud: does it rise or fall, is it straight or bent, and how tight is it? Only if it’s straight does the correlation mean what people think it means.”

Key insight

Every warning in this chapter exists for one reason: the numbers cannot see the picture. r, b₁, r², the F test and the p-value are all computed identically whether the cloud is a straight band, a banana, or a blob with one wild point.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Reading a middling r as “a weak relationship”. A strong but non-monotone curve can give r ≈ .3, and a symmetric U gives r ≈ 0 — not because the relationship is weak but because it is not a line.

Check yourself

A scatterplot of y against x rises steeply, but the points are scattered in a wide band. Is the relationship strong?
No. Steepness is the slope; strength is the tightness of the band. A steep, loose cloud has a large b₁ and a small r. They are independent pieces of information, which is exactly why the lecture asks you to report both.
r comes out at 0.04. Name two completely different situations that produce that.
(1) There really is no relationship — a formless blob. (2) There is a strong symmetric curve, such as a U: the rising half contributes positive products to SP, the falling half contributes negative ones, and they cancel. Only the plot can tell the two apart.

🔗 Measure the togetherness — r

Where the correlation coefficient actually comes from.

⬇ builds ona plot that is straight enough to summarise with one number⬆ hands upr — and, as a by-product, the two sums of squares the slope will need
  • ▸SP, the sum of products, is the engine: each person contributes (their x deviation) × (their y deviation).
  • ▸Agree with the trend — high on both, or low on both — and the product is positive. Disagree and it is negative.
  • ▸Dividing by √(SS(x)·SS(y)) strips out the units and forces r between −1 and +1.
🪝 memory hookSP is a vote count. Every person votes “the pattern goes up” or “the pattern goes down”, and the size of their vote is how unusual they are on both variables at once. r is that vote total, normalised.

In words

SP, the sum of products, is the engine of the whole chapter. Every person contributes their distance from the mean on x multiplied by their distance from the mean on y. Agree with the trend and the product is positive; disagree and it is negative. Add them up and you have SP. Dividing by √(SS(x)·SS(y)) does one job only: it strips out the units and forces the result between −1 and +1.

  • The shortcut on slide 7: SP = ΣXY − (ΣX)(ΣY)/n — the same number without computing every deviation by hand.
  • The lecture’s own dataset — family income against average grade, n = 14 — works out to r = 0.65 and r² = 0.42 (the slide prints the data and the formula, not the value).

Rules of the game for step 3

  • SP is the only part of r that can be negative. SS(x) and SS(y) are sums of squares, so the SIGN of r is entirely the sign of SP.
  • |SP| can never exceed √(SS(x)·SS(y)). An r outside [−1, 1] means you divided by the wrong thing — most often by SS(x) alone, which gives the slope.
  • r has no units. That is the whole purpose of the denominator, and the reason r is comparable across studies while b₁ is not.

Say it to a friend

“Take everyone’s distance from the average on both variables and multiply them. If someone is above average on both, that’s a positive contribution. Add them all up and you get SP. Then divide by the biggest SP the data could possibly have given, and you get a number between −1 and 1.”

Key insight

The numerator carries everything interesting — direction and strength. The denominator exists only to make r comparable across studies, by dividing out the units and the scale.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Dividing SP by SS(x) and calling it r. SP ⁄ SS(x) is b₁, the slope: a different number, with units, that can be any size at all. With SP = 40, SS(x) = 44 and SS(y) = 40 you get r = .953 but a slope of .909.

Check yourself

SP = 60, SS(x) = 100, SS(y) = 144. Give r, and give the slope.
r = 60 ⁄ √(100 × 144) = 60 ⁄ 120 = 0.50. The slope is b₁ = SP ⁄ SS(x) = 60 ⁄ 100 = 0.60. Same numerator, different denominator, completely different meaning — r is unitless, b₁ is in units of y per unit of x.
One person in a dataset is far above average on x and far below average on y. What does that person do to SP?
Contributes a large NEGATIVE product, because the two deviations have opposite signs and both are large. One such case can pull r down noticeably — which is why the lecture insists that r is not resistant, and why the plot comes first.

📏 Fit the line — b₀, b₁ and least squares

Of all the lines you could draw, why this one, and what the two numbers mean.

⬇ builds onr, SP and SS(x) from step 3⬆ hands uptwo numbers — a machine you can put an x into and get a prediction out of
  • ▸b₁, the slope: how much Y changes for each one-unit rise in X. It carries the units of both variables.
  • ▸b₀, the intercept: the predicted Y at X = 0. Meaningful only if X = 0 sits inside the data.
  • ▸Least squares picks the line with the smallest SSE = Σ(Y − Ŷ)², measured straight up and down.
🪝 memory hookA taxi fare. b₀ is the flag-drop you pay for sitting down; b₁ is the rate per kilometre. Fare = flag-drop + rate × distance is literally Ŷ = b₀ + b₁X.

In words

The line is two numbers and nothing more. b₁ says how much Y changes for each one-unit rise in X; b₀ says where the line sits when X = 0. Least squares chooses them by one criterion: of all possible lines, take the one whose squared vertical gaps add up to the smallest total. Vertical, because a vertical gap is exactly a prediction error in Y — which is the line’s whole job.

  • Slide 16 gives the slope twice: SP ⁄ SS(x) if you have the sums, r · s(y) ⁄ s(x) if you have r and the standard deviations.
  • Slide 17’s free check: (X̄, Ȳ) is always on the line. Rearranged, that is b₀ = Ȳ − b₁X̄.

Rules of the game for step 4

  • Distances are measured VERTICALLY, never perpendicular. A vertical gap is a prediction error in Y, which is what the line is for.
  • We square the residuals because the plain sum is zero for the best line and for many terrible ones. Squaring makes every miss count, and makes big misses count disproportionately.
  • Ŷ with a hat is always a prediction, never an observation. The hat is the whole distinction.

Say it to a friend

“The line is just two numbers: where you start when the predictor is zero, and how much you gain for every step up in it. And the way we pick those two numbers is simple — draw any line, measure how far each point sits above or below it, square those gaps so the misses can’t cancel out, add them up, and take the line where that total is as small as it can possibly get.”

Key insight

A slope is an average change, not a promise. “One more point of role problems predicts 9.969 more burnout” describes the trend across people — not what will happen to any particular employee.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Interpreting b₀ as if X = 0 were meaningful. In the burnout model b₀ = −44.838 — a negative burnout score, for an employee with zero role problems and zero investment. Nobody in the study was near that; the intercept is just the number that sets the height of the line.

Check yourself

A least-squares line has slope 2.5, with X̄ = 16 and Ȳ = 62. Find b₀, then check it.
b₀ = Ȳ − b₁X̄ = 62 − 2.5 × 16 = 62 − 40 = 22. The check is free: put X̄ back in and 22 + 2.5 × 16 = 62 = Ȳ ✓, because the point of averages always lies on the line. Stopping at 62, or adding instead of subtracting to get 102, are the two usual slips.
Could a different line ever beat the least-squares line?
Not on SSE — that is what “least squares” means: it is DEFINED as the line minimising Σ(Y − Ŷ)². Another line can beat it on a different criterion, such as the largest single residual or perpendicular distances. Which is exactly why the criterion has to be stated, and why one far-out point can drag the line towards it.

✅ Check what the model quietly requires

Slide 18 in four lines — and what each one actually looks like.

⬇ builds ona fitted line, and the residuals it leaves behind⬆ hands uppermission to trust every p-value and interval that follows
  • ▸Linear relation. Checked by the scatterplot and, better, by the residual plot.
  • ▸Y is normally distributed at EVERY value of X. The spread around the line is bell-shaped.
  • ▸The variance of Y is equally large at every value of X — a band of constant width (“homoscedasticity”).
🪝 memory hookPicture a garden hose lying along the line. Straight hose (1), round cross-section (2), the same diameter end to end (3), and no droplet connected to any other (4).

In words

Up to here nothing has been assumed; describing a cloud with a line needs no conditions. The moment you want a p-value, four conditions arrive. Three of them are about the residuals rather than about the variables you measured, which is why the residual plot — not a histogram of Y — is the tool that checks them. The fourth, independence, is a fact about how the data were collected and shows up in no plot at all.

  • The observations are independent: no repeated measures on one person, no clustered families, no time series.
  • Nothing here assumes X is normal, and nothing assumes Y is normal overall. Those are the two commonest misreadings of slide 18.

Rules of the game for step 5

  • The first three assumptions are about the RESIDUALS, not about the variables you measured, so the residual plot — not a histogram of Y — is what checks them. The fourth, independence, is checked by reading the method section.
  • Check the assumptions BEFORE reading any p-value. They are the conditions under which the p-value means what it says.
  • Nothing in regression assumes anything about the distribution of X. X is treated as fixed.

Say it to a friend

“The model says: at every value of the predictor, the outcomes scatter around the line in a bell shape, and that bell is the same width wherever you look. Plus everyone in the dataset is a separate case. If those hold, the p-values and intervals mean what they say.”

Key insight

An assumption violation never announces itself in the output. SPSS will happily print a slope, a t, an F and a p-value for data that break all four — which is why the checking is your job and not the software’s.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Testing whether Y is normally distributed overall. If X has a strong effect, Y can be wildly non-normal overall — bimodal, even — while the assumption holds perfectly, because the assumption is about the spread at EACH value of X.

Check yourself

Which assumption does a fan-shaped residual plot break, and which does an arch break?
A fan — residuals spreading out as x rises — breaks equal variance. An arch — positive in the middle, negative at both ends — breaks linearity: that bend is curvature the straight line failed to capture. Neither shows up in r².
A study measures the same ten children on five consecutive days and regresses child viewing on parental viewing, calling it n = 50. What is wrong?
Independence. There are ten people, not fifty independent observations, so the errors within a child are related. A df error of 48 is a fiction, the standard errors come out too small, and the p-value is too good to be true.

  • ▸A residual plot puts the residuals against x. Ideally: featureless scatter around zero.
  • ▸Any pattern — an arch, a fan, a run — signals a deviation from a linear relationship.
🪝 memory hookr² is the score; the residual plot is the replay. A team can win on the scoreboard and still have played badly — and only the replay shows you how.

In words

Two questions that sound like one. r² asks how much of the variation in Y the line accounts for — a share, with no units. The residual plot asks something different and more basic: was a straight line the right shape at all? A curved relationship can score respectably on the first and fail the second, and the exam likes exactly that case.

  • Prediction is then arithmetic: substitute x and read off ŷ. The birds give 31.9343 − 0.3040 × 60 = 13.69.
  • Predict only inside the range of x you tested. Outside it you are extrapolating a shape nobody checked.

Rules of the game for step 6

  • r² answers HOW MUCH. The residual plot answers WHETHER A STRAIGHT LINE WAS THE RIGHT SHAPE. They are different questions and can disagree.
  • r² is a proportion with no units. Multiply by 100 only to say “per cent of the variation” — never “per cent of the cases”.
  • A prediction from the line is a predicted AVERAGE for everyone at that value of x, not a promise about one case. Step 10 puts an honest interval around it.

Say it to a friend

“r² tells me what share of the differences in the outcome my predictor accounts for. But a good r² doesn’t prove the line was the right shape — for that I look at the leftovers. If the leftovers have a pattern, my straight line missed something.”

Key insight

r² and the residual plot answer different questions, and the interesting case is the one where they disagree: a strong curve, fitted with a straight line, gives a respectable r² and a residual plot with an obvious arch.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Reading r² as the percentage of PEOPLE the line gets right. It is a share of the VARIATION — of the squared distances from the mean — and no individual is predicted exactly.

🧪 Test the slope: t = b ⁄ SE

H₀: β₁ = 0, and the confidence interval that carries the same verdict.

⬇ builds onb₁ and its Std. Error from the output in step 7⬆ hands upa verdict on THIS predictor, and an interval that says it again
  • ▸H₀: β₁ = 0 says the line is flat in the population — the predictor carries no information.
  • ▸t = b₁ ⁄ SE(b₁): how many standard errors your slope sits away from zero.
  • ▸df = n − p − 1, where p is the number of predictors. One predictor → n − 2.
🪝 memory hookA slope is a signal and its standard error is the noise. t is the signal-to-noise ratio, and only the ratio decides whether you can hear anything.

In words

Here is where Lecture 5 starts earning its name. H₀: β₁ = 0 says the line is flat in the population — the predictor carries no information at all. The slope you measured would have come out differently with nine other families, and the Std. Error column says by how much. t is simply the slope divided by its own standard error: how many standard errors away from zero you landed.

  • TV study: t = .687 ⁄ .467 = 1.472 on df 7, p = .185. Not significant.
  • The 95% interval is b₁ ± t* · SE(b₁) = .687 ± 2.365 × .467 → (−0.42, 1.79). It contains zero, so p > .05.

Rules of the game for step 8

  • A slope is never judged by its size alone. 9.969 and 0.687 are not comparable; only each slope against ITS OWN standard error is.
  • df = n − p − 1. Count the predictors, not the rows of the table — the constant is not a predictor.
  • If the 95% confidence interval contains zero then p > .05, always. They are two views of one calculation, not two pieces of evidence.

Say it to a friend

“The slope I measured is 0.687, but if I’d sampled nine different families I’d have got something else. The standard error says how much it would have jumped around — about 0.467. Since my slope is only about one and a half of its own standard errors away from zero, zero is still perfectly plausible.”

Key insight

“Not significant” does not mean “no relationship”. With n = 9 the TV study simply had too little data to tell a slope of 0.687 apart from zero. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Concluding that the bigger slope is the more convincing one. The burnout model’s 9.969 (SE 2.579, t = 3.865, p = .006) is convincing; the TV study’s 0.687 (SE 0.467, t = 1.472, p = .185) is not — and those two numbers are in different units entirely, so their sizes were never comparable.

🎲 Test the whole model: split the variance

SST = SSM + SSE, and the F that follows from it.

⬇ builds onthe three sums of squares in the ANOVA table⬆ hands upone verdict on the model as a whole — the only test that survives into step 11
  • ▸Each point’s distance from Ȳ splits cleanly: (Ŷ − Ȳ) explained, plus (Y − Ŷ) left over.
  • ▸Square and sum: SST = SSM + SSE, with SSM = r² · SS(Y) and SSE = (1 − r²) · SS(Y).
  • ▸Divide each by its df: dfM = p (so 1 with one predictor), dfE = n − p − 1.
🪝 memory hookF is a value-for-money ratio. The numerator is what one predictor bought you; the denominator is what a spare observation costs. A model is worth keeping when each predictor buys more than noise alone would.

In words

The same question, asked of the model instead of the coefficient. Without a line, your best guess for anyone is Ȳ, and the total error is SST. With the line, some of that error disappears — that is SSM — and some remains, SSE. Turn each into a mean square by dividing by its own df, and F compares what the predictor bought against what error costs anyway.

  • F = MSM ⁄ MSE. TV study: (7.562 ⁄ 1) ⁄ (24.438 ⁄ 7) = 7.562 ⁄ 3.491 = 2.166, p = .185.
  • With one predictor F = t², so the two tests give the same p-value — they are one test written twice.
📌 A note worth having
On F = t²: the identity is exact in theory. Squaring the printed t gives 1.472² = 2.167 against the table’s F = 2.166; and if you recompute t yourself from the rounded B and SE you get .687 ⁄ .467 = 1.4711, whose square is 2.164. Both gaps are rounding in the printed values, not a second test — SPSS reports p = .185 for both tests.

Rules of the game for step 9

  • Turn the sums of squares into MEAN squares before you divide. SSM ÷ SSE is not F, and it is the commonest slip on this step.
  • F always carries two df, in the order (model, error) — p and n − p − 1. The two orders have completely different critical values.
  • The F test is inherently two-sided: squaring destroys the sign, so it can never tell you the DIRECTION of an effect.

Say it to a friend

“Without a model, my best guess for anyone is the mean, and the total error is SST. With the line, some of that error goes away — that’s SSM — and some remains, SSE. F compares how much the line bought me against how much error is just there anyway. If the line bought little, F is near 1 and the model isn’t earning its place.”

Key insight

In simple regression the t test and the F test are the same test, and the p-values match. F earns its keep only in multiple regression, where it tests all the predictors at once and no single t can.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Dividing the sums of squares directly: 7.562 ÷ 24.438 = 0.309, which is not F and is not anything. Each SS must first be divided by its own df.

📐 How wrong will a prediction be?

The standard error of the estimate, and the two intervals built on it.

⬇ builds onSSE and df error from the table in step 9⬆ hands upan error bar on every prediction — and two different ones, for two different questions
  • ▸s = √(SSE ⁄ df error): the average prediction error, in the units of Y. TV: √(24.438 ⁄ 7) = 1.868 hours.
  • ▸It is √MSE from the ANOVA table — the same number, reprinted in the Model Summary.
  • ▸A confidence interval for the mean response asks where the AVERAGE y sits for everyone at this x.
🪝 memory hookr² is the exam grade for your model; s is the error bar on the ruler. One is a percentage with no units, the other is hours, euros or centimetres.

In words

Significance says the line is probably not flat. It says nothing about how useful the line is. For that there is s, the standard error of the estimate: the average prediction error, in the units of Y. Put it around a prediction and you get an interval — and there are two, depending on whether you are asking about the average person at that x or about one particular person.

  • A prediction interval asks where ONE new case will land. Its standard error carries an extra 1 + inside the root, so it is always wider.
  • Both are narrowest at x̄ and fan out, because the term (x* − x̄)² is zero there and grows as you move away.

Rules of the game for step 10

  • Keep the two standard errors apart. s (Std. Error of the Estimate) is error in the PREDICTIONS; SE(b₁) is error in the COEFFICIENT. The TV study prints 1.868 and 0.467.
  • Both intervals are centred on the same ŷ. The only difference is their width, and the prediction interval is always the wider of the two.
  • Predict only inside the range of x that was tested (slide 66). Outside it, neither interval is honest.

Say it to a friend

“If I use this line to predict, how wrong will I typically be? Square the residuals, average them over the leftover degrees of freedom, square-root back — about 1.87 hours here. And if you ask me about the average child of 2.5-hour parents I only have to worry about pinning down the line; if you ask me about YOUR child I also have to allow for children differing from the line, and that answer is always wider.”

Key insight

Collect more data and the confidence interval shrinks towards the line — but the prediction interval never shrinks below roughly ±t* · s. You can learn the line perfectly and still predict an individual badly, because individuals genuinely differ.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Quoting the Std. Error column next to B as “how wrong my predictions will be”. That column is the wobble in the coefficient across repeated samples. The prediction error is the Std. Error of the Estimate — a different table, a different unit.

In words

Real outcomes have several causes, and those causes overlap. Ten employees; role problems and personal investment, both on 0–15, predicting burnout on 0–100. The complication is stated up front on slide 40: the two predictors are related to each other. Everything odd about multiple regression flows from that one fact — and it is why each coefficient now answers a careful, conditional question.

  • R = .939 is the correlation between PREDICTED and OBSERVED burnout — not between burnout and either predictor. R² = .882.

Rules of the game for step 11

  • Every coefficient in a multiple regression is a PARTIAL slope: the effect of that predictor with the others held constant. Say the “holding constant” clause out loud, or the sentence is wrong.
  • Compare importance with the Betas, never with the raw b’s — a raw b carries the units of its own predictor.
  • df error = n − p − 1, and p now counts every predictor. Ten cases and two predictors gives df error = 7.

Say it to a friend

🪜 Does the extra block pay? R² change

Hierarchical regression, and the one test that stops you fooling yourself.

⬇ builds onan R² for a model you already have⬆ hands upa decision — keep the extra predictors, or throw them out
  • ▸Hierarchical regression enters predictors in BLOCKS, in an order you chose for theoretical reasons, and asks what each block adds.
  • ▸The hypotheses: H₀: R² of model 1 = R² of model 2 — that is, H₀: R²change = 0.

🪝 memory hookR² is a ratchet — it only turns one way. Adding a predictor can never lower it, which is exactly why “it went up” proves nothing and you need a test of how much.

In words

The last step of the lecture, and the most disciplined. Slide 76 asks whether burnout can be predicted better still by adding age and gender. R² duly rises, from .882 to .922. But R² ALWAYS rises when you add predictors, even useless ones, so the rise has to be tested rather than reported. F change = 1.280 on df 2 and 5 gives p = .356, and the lecturer throws the two variables out.

  • Conclusion in the lecture’s own words: adding age and gender does not significantly improve the prediction. They go.
  • Adjusted R² barely moves, .848 → .860 — a rise far too small to be a recommendation, which is exactly what adjusted R² is for.
📌 A note worth having
Why the denominator df is 5 and not 7: model 2 has four predictors, so n − p − 1 = 10 − 4 − 1 = 5. The error df in an F change test always belongs to the LARGER model.

Rules of the game for step 12

  • R² can never fall when you add a predictor. That is arithmetic, not evidence — which is exactly why the rise has to be TESTED.
  • The F change test has df (number of predictors ADDED, n − total predictors − 1). Here 2 and 5, not 2 and 7.
  • Adjusted R² CAN fall, and that is the point of it. If it drops when you add a block, the block was worse than nothing.

Say it to a friend

“I had a model with two predictors explaining 88% of burnout. I threw in age and gender and it went to 92%. But adding anything at all pushes that number up, even random numbers. The test asks whether a 4-point rise is more than you’d expect from two junk predictors — and here it wasn’t.”

Key insight

Adjusted R² and the F change test exist for one reason: the raw R² is structurally incapable of telling you that a predictor was useless. Two columns of random numbers would also have raised it.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Reporting the .040 rise as an improvement. The test is what decides, and p = .356 says a rise that size is no more than two junk predictors would have produced.

  • d.It equals SS(x) when x and y are the same variable, and is otherwise undefined
SP = Σ(X−X̄)(Y−Ȳ), and a product of two deviations is negative whenever they have opposite signs. SS(x) and SS(y) cannot be negative, which is why the sign of r is entirely the sign of SP.
The other options
✗a. Only r is bounded by ±1. SP carries the product of the two units and can be any size at all.
✗c. SP is a sum of PRODUCTS of two different deviations, not a sum of squares — the two deviations can have opposite signs.
✗d. SP is well defined for any two variables. It does reduce to SS(x) when y = x, but that is a special case, not a restriction.
🏷️ SS can never be negative; SP can. That one asymmetry is where the sign of r comes from.
